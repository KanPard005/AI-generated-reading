## Why Kant matters

Immanuel Kant is the pivot of modern Western philosophy. Before him, the central quarrel was between rationalists such as Descartes, Leibniz, and Christian Wolff, who held that reason alone can reveal the basic structure of reality, and empiricists such as Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, who held that all substantive knowledge comes from experience. Kant argued that both camps misdescribed how knowledge works, and he built a system that kept what he took to be right in each.

His method is called *critical*. Rather than asking directly what the world is like, he asks what reason can legitimately do, and what conditions make experience, science, morality, and judgment possible at all. "Critique" in his titles means examination of limits and warrant, not fault-finding.

The main results, in brief:

- **Knowledge.** We know objects as they appear to us, structured by the forms of our own cognition (space, time, and a set of pure concepts called categories), not as they are in themselves. This secures the necessity of mathematics and Newtonian science, and it rules out traditional metaphysics' claims to theoretical knowledge of God, the soul, and the world as a whole.
- **Morality.** The moral law is a demand of pure practical reason. It binds unconditionally, and its authority comes from the autonomy of rational agents, not from consequences, feelings, or divine command.
- **Freedom.** Freedom is compatible with universal natural causation, because the two apply at different standpoints.
- **Judgment.** Judgments of beauty and the sublime, and our way of understanding living things, reveal a distinctive faculty that links nature and freedom.
- **Politics.** Legitimate states protect equal external freedom under law, and the proper long-term goal is a federation of republican states at peace.

## Life and times

Kant was born on 22 April 1724 in Königsberg, the capital of East Prussia (today Kaliningrad, Russia), into a modest family. His father was a harness-maker. His mother and the family's Pietist church shaped his early moral education. Pietism stressed inner sincerity and the moral quality of the heart over doctrine and ritual. Kant later disliked its emotionalism and its schooling, but the emphasis on the moral worth of one's inner disposition stays with him.

He attended the Collegium Fridericianum, a Latin-heavy Pietist school, and entered the University of Königsberg in 1740, where Martin Knutzen introduced him to Newtonian physics and the Leibnizian-Wolffian school philosophy. After his father's death in 1746 he worked for about nine years as a private tutor, then took his master's degree in 1755 and began lecturing as a *Privatdozent*, paid directly by student fees.

For fifteen years he taught logic, metaphysics, ethics, mathematics, physics, and physical geography and anthropology. His early scientific work was serious: *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens* (1755) proposed that the solar system formed from a rotating nebula. In 1770, after declining chairs elsewhere, he was appointed professor of logic and metaphysics at Königsberg.

His inaugural dissertation that year first stated the claim that space and time are forms of human sensibility rather than features of things in themselves. Then came a long silence. Between 1770 and 1781 he published little while working out the system, and then produced the *Critique of Pure Reason* in 1781 at the age of 57. It was dense, unusually structured, and poorly understood at first. A hostile early review that classed it with Berkeley's idealism prompted Kant to write the shorter *Prolegomena* (1783) as an accessible summary, and to revise the Critique substantially for its second edition in 1787. Scholars cite the two editions as A (1781) and B (1787).

The decade after the first Critique was highly productive: the major works on ethics, the second and third Critiques, and essays on history and politics. In 1794, after he published *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*, the Prussian king Frederick William II reprimanded him and ordered him to stop writing on religion. Kant complied while the king lived, stopped lecturing in 1796, and died on 12 February 1804. His reported last words were "Es ist gut" ("It is good").

Two popular images need correcting. Kant was no dull recluse: he was a sociable host who followed the news of the French Revolution closely. He did rarely leave the vicinity of Königsberg, but the legend that neighbors set their clocks by his daily walk is probably exaggerated.

## The problem he inherited

Three facts framed the problem Kant set himself.

**First, Newtonian physics worked.** It delivered laws that seemed both universal and necessary, and it was spectacularly successful. Any epistemology had to account for how such knowledge is possible.

**Second, Hume's analysis of causation.** Hume argued that we never perceive a necessary connection between a cause and its effect. We observe only that events of one kind regularly follow events of another, and the idea of necessity comes from a habit of expectation. If so, the necessity claimed by science has no rational foundation. Kant said Hume "awakened me from my dogmatic slumber," meaning the unreflective rationalism he had absorbed from the Wolffians.

**Third, metaphysics was in disarray.** Rationalist metaphysics claimed to prove from pure reason that the soul is immortal, that the world has a beginning, that God exists, but the proofs never settled anything. Kant called it a battlefield of endless controversies, in contrast to mathematics and natural science, which steadily progress.

So his guiding question was whether metaphysics could become a secure science, and he reformulated it as: *how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?*

### Two distinctions

The question uses two distinctions that cut across each other.

- **A priori versus a posteriori** concerns how a judgment can be known. A priori knowledge is justified independently of experience, and so is necessary and universal. A posteriori knowledge depends on experience.
- **Analytic versus synthetic** concerns what the judgment says. In an analytic judgment the predicate is already contained in the concept of the subject ("all bodies are extended"), so denying it is a contradiction. In a synthetic judgment the predicate adds something not contained in the subject concept ("all bodies are heavy").

**Kant's classification of judgments, with examples**

|  | A priori | A posteriori |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** | "All bodies are extended." "Bachelors are unmarried." Uncontroversial. | Kant allows none; analytic truths need no experience. |
| **Synthetic** | "7 + 5 = 12." Geometric theorems. "Every event has a cause." "In all changes of the material world, the quantity of matter remains constant." **The contested and crucial category.** | "The cat is on the mat." "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level." Ordinary empirical claims. |

Empiricists tended to say there are only analytic a priori truths (relations of ideas) and synthetic a posteriori truths (matters of fact). Rationalists tended to treat all a priori knowledge as analytic, derivable from definitions by logic. Kant argued that mathematics and the foundations of physics are *synthetic* and yet *a priori*. His example: the concept of "7 + 5" contains only the idea of combining the two numbers, and nowhere in it is the concept of 12; you reach 12 by constructing the sum in intuition, for example with fingers or points. Likewise "the shortest path between two points is a straight line" adds a notion of magnitude to the concept of straightness.

If such judgments exist, something must explain how we can know, independent of experience, truths that go beyond mere concept analysis. That explanation is what the rest of the Critique supplies. Later philosophers (Frege, Quine) questioned whether his definition of "analytic" is too narrow, or whether the distinction holds at all.

## The Copernican revolution

Kant's solution is a reversal of perspective that he compared to Copernicus. Until then, he says, it was assumed that our cognition must conform to objects. But on that assumption it is hard to see how we could know anything about objects a priori, since experience has not yet given them to us. So he proposes to try the opposite: *objects must conform to our cognition*.

The idea is that experience is not a passive reception of how things are. It is the product of two contributions: the world supplies the raw material of sensation, and the mind supplies the forms in which that material is ordered and the concepts under which it is understood. What we know a priori about objects is only what we ourselves put into them, which is why it can be known independently of any particular experience: it holds of every object of possible experience for beings constituted like us.

> **The price.** This position, called *transcendental idealism*, comes with a cost. If the forms of experience originate in us, then what we know are *appearances*, objects as they present themselves to us, not *things in themselves*. Kant accepts this cost willingly. It explains how a priori knowledge of nature is possible, and it also limits what reason can claim.
{: .note}

A note on vocabulary. "Transcendental" does not mean "transcending experience." It refers to inquiry into the a priori conditions that make experience possible. What goes beyond the bounds of experience he calls "transcendent," and that is exactly what he thinks theoretical reason cannot legitimately do.

### Three faculties

The structure of the first Critique follows Kant's division of our cognitive powers.

- **Sensibility** is receptive: it gives us *intuitions*, immediate representations of particular things. Its pure forms are space and time.
- **Understanding** is spontaneous: it supplies *concepts*, and judges by applying them. Its pure concepts are the categories.
- **Reason** in the narrow sense is the faculty of inference, which seeks the unconditioned and generates *ideas* such as the soul, the world-whole, and God.

His slogan for the first two: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind." Knowledge requires both, and neither alone suffices. This is his compromise between the rationalist overemphasis on concepts and the empiricist overemphasis on sense data.

## Space and time

The first part of the Critique, the Transcendental Aesthetic, argues that space and time are neither things in themselves nor concepts abstracted from experience. They are the *pure forms of intuition*: the ways in which our sensibility necessarily presents objects to us. Space is the form of outer sense (how things appear outside us and beside one another), and time is the form of inner sense (how our own states appear to us), and by extension of everything we experience.

### The arguments

- **Presupposition.** To represent something as outside me, or as at a different place from another thing, I must already have the representation of space. So space cannot have been derived from outer experience.
- **Necessity.** We can imagine space with no objects in it, but we cannot imagine objects with no space. Space is therefore a necessary condition of outer appearances, not a product of them. The same holds for time.
- **Intuition, not concept.** We represent space as a single, infinite, given whole. The "many spaces" are only parts of the one space, not instances falling under a general concept. A representation with that structure is an intuition.

### The payoff

These claims explain synthetic a priori knowledge in mathematics. Geometry is possible a priori because it describes the structure of our form of outer intuition. Arithmetic and the theory of magnitudes are tied to time: counting is a successive synthesis. Since all objects of experience must be given through these forms, mathematics applies with necessity to everything we can experience, and that explains why it fits nature so well.

Kant sums up the position as *empirical realism* combined with *transcendental idealism*. Objects in space and time are fully real as objects of experience, and the table in front of you is not an illusion. But space and time themselves, and so the spatiotemporal objects, are not features of reality independent of our cognition.

> **Objections.** The "Trendelenburg gap": Kant's argument shows space is a form of our intuition but does not obviously exclude that it is *also* a feature of things in themselves. And non-Euclidean geometry and relativity lead many to deny that Euclidean geometry is the necessary form of experience; defenders reply that Kant's claim concerns the form of intuition, not physical space as such.

## The understanding and its categories

The Transcendental Analytic is the heart of the first Critique. Space and time give us a manifold of appearances, but experience is more than a stream of sensations: it is a coherent awareness of objects that persist, interact, and obey laws. For this to occur the understanding must organize intuitions under pure concepts, the *categories*.

### Where the categories come from

In the *metaphysical deduction*, Kant claims that the understanding's basic concepts correspond to the logical forms of judgment (as listed by traditional logic). If the understanding is the faculty of judging, then the ways it can judge reveal the ways it can unify a manifold. This yields twelve categories in four groups.

**Judgments and categories**

| Group | Forms of judgment | Categories |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | Universal, particular, singular | Unity, plurality, totality |
| **Quality** | Affirmative, negative, infinite | Reality, negation, limitation |
| **Relation** | Categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive | Substance and accident; cause and effect; community (reciprocity) |
| **Modality** | Problematic, assertoric, apodictic | Possibility, existence, necessity |

Many readers find the derivation forced, and most of the interest has gone to the next step.

### The transcendental deduction

The legal metaphor is deliberate. A *quid juris* question asks by what right we apply these concepts to experience. Concepts like "dog" are learned from experience, but the categories are not. The skeptic can ask why nature should follow our concepts of substance and causation. This is Hume's challenge.

Kant's answer, in outline:

1. To have experience of an object, the manifold of intuition must be combined. Combination is not given by the senses; it is an act of the understanding.
2. For my representations to be mine, I must be able to accompany all of them with "I think." This standing possibility of self-ascription is the *transcendental unity of apperception*.
3. That unity of consciousness can be achieved only if my representations are combined according to rules that make them represent an object, something that holds together independently of my passing perceptions.
4. The categories are precisely those rules. Therefore the categories necessarily apply to everything that can be an object of experience for us.

This reverses Hume. Causation is not a feature we infer from experience; it is a condition for having experience of an objective world in the first place. The argument has two versions (A and B) that differ in emphasis and structure, and its exact logic is the most debated question in Kant scholarship.

### Schematism and the principles

Categories are pure concepts, and sensations are particular, so how can the former apply to the latter? Kant's answer is the *schematism*: the imagination produces time-determinations that mediate between category and appearance. The schema of substance is permanence in time, and the schema of cause is regular succession in time. The categories only have meaning, and thus legitimate use, for objects of possible experience.

The result is a set of synthetic a priori principles of pure understanding, which in Kant's view are the foundations of Newtonian science:

- **Axioms of Intuition.** All intuitions are extensive magnitudes, which is why mathematics applies to experience.
- **Anticipations of Perception.** Sensations have intensive magnitude (degree) and vary continuously.
- **Analogies of Experience.** (1) Substance persists through change. (2) Every alteration has a cause, in accordance with a law. (3) Substances that coexist are in thoroughgoing interaction.
- **Postulates of Empirical Thought.** The criteria for what counts as possible, actual, and necessary in experience.

### The Second Analogy in a nutshell

This argument is the cleanest example of Kant answering Hume. My perceptions are always successive. When I look at a house, I may scan from roof to foundation; the order is mine to choose, and I do not conclude that the parts of the house follow one another. When I watch a ship move downstream, the order of my perceptions (upstream position, then downstream position) is fixed, and I take it to reflect an objective event. What distinguishes the two cases? Only that in the second I regard the order as determined by a rule: the later state necessarily follows the earlier. So I can represent an objective *event* at all only by presupposing causal law. Hume was right that necessity cannot be read off from observation; Kant argues that it is built into the very possibility of experiencing an objective temporal order.

## Appearances and things in themselves

The claim that we know only appearances raises an obvious question: what are things in themselves? Kant's position is subtle and controversial.

He uses "noumenon" in two senses. In the *negative* sense, a noumenon is just a thing considered as not an object of our sensible intuition; it is a boundary concept that marks the limits of sensibility. In the *positive* sense, it would be an object of a non-sensible, intellectual intuition, a faculty that creates its objects by thinking them, such as a divine mind might have. Humans have no such faculty, so we cannot know any noumenon in the positive sense. We can think things in themselves without contradiction, but we cannot know anything about them.

The concept is needed because "appearance" implies something that appears. And, as we will see, it is also what makes room for freedom and morality.

### Two readings

Interpreters disagree on what this doctrine amounts to, and the disagreement has structured Kant scholarship for two centuries.

- **Two-world reading.** There are two sets of entities: appearances (mental or mind-dependent) and distinct things in themselves behind them. This is the older, traditional view, and it faces the problem that Kant says things in themselves "affect" our senses, which seems to apply the category of causality outside experience.
- **Two-aspect reading.** There is one set of objects considered in two ways: as they appear to beings with our cognitive constitution, and as they are independently of that constitution. Appearance and thing in itself are two standpoints on the same thing. This reading is associated with Henry Allison and others, and is common among contemporary Anglophone interpreters.

Neither reading is free of difficulty.

## The illusions of reason

If the Analytic shows what understanding can legitimately do, the Transcendental Dialectic shows what happens when reason overreaches. Reason seeks the *unconditioned*: for every conditioned thing, it demands the full series of conditions, and a final ground. This drive produces three ideas, the soul (the unconditioned subject), the world-whole (the unconditioned totality of appearances), and God (the unconditioned ground of everything). But no possible experience corresponds to them, so applying our categories to them yields illusion.

Kant stresses that this "transcendental illusion" is natural and unavoidable. Like the moon looking larger near the horizon, it persists even when you know better. Critical philosophy cannot eliminate it, only expose it so that we are not deceived.

### Paralogisms: the soul

Rational psychology (Descartes and the Wolffians) tried to prove from the thought "I think" that the self is a simple, persisting, immaterial substance. Kant says this is a fallacy of equivocation. The "I" of apperception is a formal unity that accompanies all thought; it is not an object given in intuition, so we have no basis for ascribing substance, simplicity, or immortality to it. We know ourselves only as appearances in inner sense, and know nothing of what we are in ourselves.

### Antinomies: the world

Here reason contradicts itself. Kant presents four pairs of arguments, each with a thesis and an antithesis that seem equally compelling.

**The four antinomies**

| Topic | Thesis | Antithesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | The world has a beginning in time and is limited in space. | The world is infinite in time and space. |
| **Quality** | Every composite substance consists of simple parts. | There are no simple parts. |
| **Relation** | There is causality through freedom in addition to natural causality. | There is only natural causality. |
| **Modality** | A necessary being belongs to the world. | No necessary being exists in or outside the world. |

Kant's diagnosis is that the dispute rests on a shared false assumption: that the world is a given thing in itself with determinate size and composition. If the world is only appearance, it is not given as a completed whole, but only as a task of ongoing empirical regress. For the first two (mathematical) antinomies, both sides are therefore false. For the last two (dynamical) antinomies, both can be true, if thesis and antithesis concern different standpoints: the antithesis holds for appearances, and the thesis may hold for things as they are in themselves. This is how he makes room for freedom, discussed below.

### The ideal of pure reason: God

Kant examines the three classic proofs of God's existence.

- **Ontological** (Anselm, Descartes): God, as the most perfect being, must exist. Kant replies that "existence is not a real predicate": adding existence to a concept does not add content to it. A hundred real thalers contain no more than a hundred possible ones; the difference is whether they are actually in my purse.
- **Cosmological** (from contingent things to a necessary being): this secretly relies on the ontological argument, since it must still show that the necessary being is the most perfect being.
- **Physico-theological** (the argument from design): the most respectable, but at best it shows a world-architect who shaped pre-existing material, not an omnipotent creator.

Theoretical reason can therefore neither prove nor refute God's existence. "I had to deny knowledge," Kant says, "in order to make room for faith." The ideas still have a legitimate, *regulative* role: they direct inquiry toward systematic unity, encouraging us to look for connections and to treat nature *as if* it were an intelligible whole. They are guides for science, not claims about reality.

## Moral philosophy

Kant's ethics, developed chiefly in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), seeks the supreme principle of morality and argues that it can be known a priori. If moral requirements are necessary and universal, he reasons, they cannot be derived from contingent facts about human desires or from what experience shows us about human nature.

### The good will

The Groundwork begins with the claim that the only thing good without qualification is a *good will*. Intelligence, wit, courage, and even happiness can all be used for bad ends, and the virtues can be corrupted. A good will is good not because of what it achieves but in itself: even if it accomplishes nothing, "it would still shine like a jewel for its own sake."

### Acting from duty

An action has moral worth only when it is done *from duty*, not merely in accordance with duty. Kant's example is a shopkeeper who gives correct change to an inexperienced customer. If he does so only because honesty is good for business, his act conforms with duty but has no moral worth. Likewise, helping others from natural sympathy is amiable but not distinctively moral; the clearest case is someone whose sympathy has vanished and who still helps because it is right.

Three clarifications. Kant does not call inclinations bad; he locates moral worth in the motive of duty. He does not say consequences are irrelevant to what we ought to do, only that they do not fix the *worth* of the will. And acting from duty means acting from *respect for the moral law*, a feeling that reason itself produces.

### Maxims and imperatives

Actions flow from *maxims*, the subjective principles on which we act ("I will do A in circumstances C in order to achieve E"). The moral question is whether a maxim is acceptable. Imperatives, which express how reason constrains a will that is not automatically rational, come in two kinds.

- **Hypothetical imperatives** command conditionally: "If you want X, do Y." They cover skill and prudence. If you drop the end, the command lapses.
- **Categorical imperatives** command unconditionally, whatever you happen to want. Morality, Kant argues, must take this form, or it would be hostage to our contingent desires.

### The Formula of Universal Law

> Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.
>
> — *Groundwork, 4:421 (paraphrased translation)*
{: .formula}

This yields a test for maxims. First, formulate the maxim. Second, imagine it as a law that everyone follows. Third, check for a contradiction. Kant distinguishes two kinds.

- **Contradiction in conception.** The universalized maxim cannot even be conceived. Take false promising: "I will make a promise I do not intend to keep, to get money." If everyone did this, no one would trust promises, and the institution of promising would collapse, and with it the possibility of the maxim. These violate *perfect* duties, which admit no exceptions.
- **Contradiction in the will.** The universalized maxim is conceivable but cannot be consistently willed. Take refusing to help anyone in need: we can conceive such a world, but a finite rational agent cannot will it, since we inevitably will to have the help of others sometimes. These violate *imperfect* duties, which set ends we must adopt (developing one's talents, helping others) but leave latitude about how and when.

### Other formulations

Kant claims that several formulations express the same law from different angles.

**Formulations of the categorical imperative**

| Formulation | Statement (paraphrase) | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Universal Law (and Law of Nature)** | Act on maxims you can will as universal laws, as if they were laws of nature. | Consistency; moral reasons are reasons for everyone. |
| **Humanity** | Treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means. | Rational beings have dignity, not price. |
| **Autonomy** | Act so that your will can regard itself as giving universal law through its maxims. | The moral law is self-legislated. |
| **Kingdom of Ends** | Act as a lawmaking member of a community of ends. | A moral ideal where all rational beings' ends are harmonized. |

The **Formula of Humanity** is probably the most influential in modern ethics and law. Things have a *price* and can be replaced by equivalents; persons have *dignity*, an unconditional worth that is beyond price. The qualifier "merely" matters: you may use a taxi driver as a means of transport, but you wrongly treat him merely as a means if you deceive or coerce him in ways he could not consent to. A false promise fails because the person deceived cannot share the end you pursue, and so is used as an instrument.

The **Formula of Autonomy** contains Kant's deepest idea. Earlier moral theories grounded morality in something external: God's commands, social convention, nature, or the pursuit of happiness. Kant calls all such views *heteronomous*. On his view rational agents are the authors of the moral law. It binds us categorically exactly because it is not imposed by anything outside our own reason, and obeying it is acting freely.

## Freedom and the highest good

For Kant, morality presupposes freedom. If we ought to do something, we must be able to do it ("ought implies can"), so the moral law makes sense only for beings who can act independently of their desires.

He distinguishes two senses. *Negative* freedom is independence from determination by sensuous impulses. *Positive* freedom is the capacity of pure reason to be practical, that is, to give itself a law and act on it. Autonomy is positive freedom.

The theoretical Critique has already shown that freedom is not *impossible*: the Third Antinomy's resolution allows that a person, as an appearance, is fully determined by natural causes, while, as an agent regarded in terms of the intelligible world, she may act freely. But theoretical reason cannot prove freedom's reality. In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), Kant argues that we have a direct awareness of the moral law, which he calls a "fact of reason." Our consciousness of being bound by it shows that we are free. Many commentators regard this move as the weakest and most debated step in his ethics, since the Groundwork had attempted a different, deductive argument.

### The highest good and the postulates

Virtue is the supreme good, but not the whole good: a complete good would be virtue together with proportionate happiness. Yet nothing in nature guarantees that moral conduct leads to happiness. Kant argues that, to pursue the highest good without absurdity, practical reason must *postulate* three things: freedom, the immortality of the soul (an endless progress toward moral perfection), and the existence of God (the guarantor of the proportion of happiness to virtue).

These are not theoretical knowledge but objects of *rational faith*, justified by practical need. Kant thereby preserves a place for religious belief without returning to the metaphysics he has criticized.

## Beauty, the sublime, and purpose

The third Critique, the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790), addresses a gap Kant sees between nature, governed by necessary causal law, and freedom, governed by the moral law. He argues that a distinct faculty of *reflective judgment*, which finds a universal for a given particular, rather than applying an existing universal, bridges the gap. It appears in two domains: aesthetic judgment and teleological judgment.

### The judgment of taste

When I say "this rose is beautiful," I express a feeling of pleasure, so the judgment is subjective. Yet I speak as if beauty were a property of the rose, and I expect others to agree. Kant calls this the paradox of taste and analyzes it in four "moments."

- **Disinterested.** The pleasure in the beautiful does not depend on desire for the object's existence or use. This separates the beautiful from the merely agreeable (the pleasure of good wine) and the good.
- **Universal without a concept.** The judgment claims everyone's agreement, though no rule or proof can compel it.
- **Purposiveness without purpose.** The object seems designed for our cognitive faculties, though no particular purpose is served.
- **Necessary.** The claim is that everyone *ought* to agree, grounded in a presupposed common sense (*sensus communis*).

What underlies this is the *free play* of imagination and understanding. In ordinary cognition, imagination serves the understanding's concepts. In an experience of beauty, the two faculties harmonize without a determining concept, and the felt harmony is the pleasure. Since these faculties are the same in everyone, the pleasure can be demanded of all. Kant distinguishes *free beauty* (flowers, abstract patterns) from *dependent beauty* (a building, which is judged also by its purpose), and treats fine art as the product of *genius*, an innate gift that gives the rule to art.

### The sublime

The sublime arises before the vast or powerful, and it mixes displeasure with pleasure. In the *mathematical* sublime (the starry sky, the ocean) imagination fails to grasp the magnitude, yet reason can think the infinite, so we feel the superiority of our rational vocation. In the *dynamical* sublime (storms, volcanoes) nature threatens us, and from safety we sense that our moral independence cannot be overpowered by it. The sublime thus points toward our moral nature, and beauty is likewise a "symbol of morality."

### Teleology and living things

In the second half, Kant asks how to understand organisms. An organism's parts exist both by means of and for the sake of the whole, each producing and maintaining the others; Kant calls this a *natural purpose*. We cannot make sense of such things without the concept of purpose, but it must serve as a regulative principle of reflection, not a claim that nature has designs. He doubted there could ever be a "Newton of a blade of grass."

## Politics, history, and religion

### Enlightenment

In "What Is Enlightenment?" (1784), Kant defines enlightenment as humanity's emergence from self-incurred immaturity, meaning the inability to use one's understanding without another's guidance. The motto is *Sapere aude*: dare to know, have the courage to use your own understanding. He distinguishes the *public use* of reason, as a scholar addressing the reading public, which must be free, from the *private use*, in an official post such as an officer or clergyman, which may legitimately be restricted. In "Idea for a Universal History" he reads history as the gradual development of rational capacities, driven in part by "unsocial sociability," the competitiveness that pushes us to cultivate our talents and form lawful societies.

### Right and the state

In the *Doctrine of Right*, Kant starts from a single innate right: freedom, meaning independence from being constrained by another's choice, insofar as it can coexist with everyone's freedom under universal law. Right concerns only external actions and is compatible with coercion, since hindering a hindrance to freedom is itself consistent with freedom.

In a state of nature, rights (especially property) are only provisional, because no one has authority to settle disputes, so we are obliged to enter a civil condition with public law and enforcement. The social contract is a regulative idea, a test of whether a law could be agreed to by a whole people, not a historical event. Kant favored a republican constitution with separation of powers and representation, and, controversially, denied a right of revolution: the sovereign may be criticized publicly but not resisted.

### Perpetual peace

"Toward Perpetual Peace" (1795) is framed as a mock treaty (the title comes from a Dutch inn sign showing a graveyard). Its definitive articles are that the constitution of every state should be republican, that international right should be founded on a federation of free states, and that cosmopolitan right should be limited to universal hospitality, the right of a stranger not to be treated with hostility. Kant argues that republics, whose citizens bear the costs of war, are less prone to wage it. This is a forerunner of democratic peace theory, and the essay is often cited as an ancestor of the League of Nations and the United Nations. He also condemns European colonial conquest in the essay.

> **A necessary caveat.** Kant's moral universalism coexists with views that are racist and sexist by any modern standard. His lectures on anthropology and geography, and some published essays, rank human "races" hierarchically, and he excluded women and dependent laborers from full "active" citizenship. Contemporary scholars (for example Charles Mills, Robert Bernasconi, and Pauline Kleingeld) debate how these views relate to his core philosophy. Kleingeld argues that Kant revised his racial views in the 1790s toward a more consistent universalism. Whether his philosophy can be separated from these commitments is a live question.
{: .note}

### Religion

*Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793) interprets religion through morality. Kant argues that humans have a propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-love, which he calls *radical evil*. It is not a sinful nature inherited from Adam, but a universal tendency that arises from our free choice of maxims, so we are responsible for it. Overcoming it requires a "revolution" in one's disposition, not just gradual reform. Religion in the true sense is the recognition of our moral duties as divine commands; the historical content of Scripture and church practice has value only insofar as it serves moral improvement. The Prussian censors saw the work as reducing Christianity to ethics.

## Legacy and criticism

### Immediate reception

Early critics such as Jacobi attacked the thing in itself. Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel responded by eliminating it and building systems of idealism; Hegel also criticized Kant's dualisms and his supposedly empty ethical formalism. Schopenhauer took the appearance/thing-in-itself distinction as the foundation of his own system, identifying the thing in itself with Will.

### Later movements

- **Neo-Kantianism** (Cohen, Natorp, Cassirer at Marburg; Windelband and Rickert in the southwest) dominated German philosophy in the late nineteenth century and treated Kant as a philosopher of the foundations of science and culture.
- **Analytic philosophy.** P. F. Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense* (1966) revived interest in transcendental arguments, which show that some feature is a condition of experience. Sellars, Putnam, and others took up Kantian themes. Challenges came from Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction and from modern physics. Philosophers of science developed "relativized a priori" frameworks (Reichenbach, Friedman) to retain Kant's insight that some principles constitute the framework of inquiry.
- **Ethics and politics.** Rawls's constructivism and original position, Habermas's discourse ethics, Scanlon's contractualism, and Korsgaard's, O'Neill's, and Herman's work are all heavily Kantian. The concept of inviolable human dignity in contemporary human rights law, including the opening article of Germany's Basic Law, owes much to the Formula of Humanity.

### Standard objections to Kant's ethics

- **Emptiness.** Hegel argued the universalization test is purely formal and can justify nearly anything (or condemn it), depending on how the maxim is described. Defenders reply that the test is not the whole theory and that maxims should be described at the level of agents' real intentions.
- **Rigorism.** In his reply to Benjamin Constant, Kant seemed to hold that one may not lie even to a murderer asking where a friend is hiding. Many consider this a reductio; others, such as Korsgaard, defend more flexible readings.
- **Moral psychology.** Schiller joked that Kant implies one should despise one's friends in order to help them with a clear conscience. Bernard Williams argued that Kantian impartiality alienates agents from their personal commitments (the "one thought too many"), and care ethicists argue that the tradition undervalues relationships and emotion.

## Where to read next

A sensible order, from most to least accessible:

1. **Short essays:** "What Is Enlightenment?", "Idea for a Universal History," and "Toward Perpetual Peace." Each is a few dozen pages and clear.
2. **Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals.** A short, compressed, but self-contained book and the best entry to his ethics.
3. **Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics.** A more accessible statement of the theoretical philosophy, written in a different order than the first Critique.
4. **Critique of Pure Reason.** Read the two Prefaces and the Introduction first, then the Aesthetic, the Transcendental Deduction (the B version first), the Analogies, and the Dialectic's treatment of the Paralogisms, the Antinomies, and the ideal of reason. Do not try to read it straight through on the first pass.
5. **Critique of Practical Reason** and **Critique of the Power of Judgment.**

The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant (translators including Paul Guyer, Allen Wood, and Mary Gregor) is the standard English series. Scholars cite by the original page numbers (A/B for the first Critique, Akademie pagination for the rest), printed in the margins of most translations.

For secondary reading, commonly recommended works include Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense*, Henry Allison's *Kant's Transcendental Idealism*, and Christine Korsgaard's *Creating the Kingdom of Ends*, along with the Kant entries in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
